Fieldwork is central to how I develop and test questions about ecology, behavior, and evolution. Observing organisms in their natural habitats helps me connect patterns measured in the laboratory or museum with the interactions that may have shaped them.

## Madagascar: pelican spiders

My current fieldwork focuses on pelican spiders (Archaeidae) in eastern Madagascar. Working across sites including Ranomafana, Andasibe–Mantadia, Maromizaha, and Ambohitantely, I investigate how closely related predators use their habitats and coexist.

I document where spiders occur, the vegetation and microhabitats they occupy, and the ecological contexts in which different species and populations are found. Field observations and collections provide the foundation for complementary studies of morphology, prey capture, muscle anatomy, and venom glands.

A key goal is to understand whether variation among populations reflects differences in ecological interactions, geographic history, or both. Studying spiders across multiple locations helps place experimental and anatomical results in a broader natural context.

## Argentina: arachnid behavior and natural history

My research training in Argentina established my interest in observing arachnids in the field and connecting their natural history with questions about reproductive behavior and evolution. Field-based perspectives continue to inform my work on scorpions and other arachnids.

## From the field to the collection

I value careful specimen documentation and museum collections. Connecting observations, locality information, images, and preserved specimens makes it possible to revisit findings and develop new comparative questions.
