I study how ecological and reproductive interactions shape behavior, morphology, and evolutionary divergence. My research combines field observations, behavioral experiments, geometric morphometrics, comparative analyses, and three-dimensional imaging. Although arachnids are my principal study systems, the questions extend to the evolution of animal form and function more broadly.

## Current research

### Ecological and functional divergence in Malagasy pelican spiders

**With Hannah Wood · Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History**

Why do closely related predators differ, and how can they coexist? I study pelican spiders of the *Eriauchenus workmani* species complex across sites in Madagascar that differ in species composition and ecological context. My work connects where spiders occur and how they use their habitat with prey capture, external morphology, internal anatomy, and evolutionary divergence.

I combine field sampling and natural history observations with behavioral experiments, high-speed video, geometric morphometrics, and micro-CT reconstructions. A central goal is to understand whether differences among populations are associated with how these spiders encounter, capture, and process prey.

### Behavior and morphology of harvestmen

**With Eileen Hebets and collaborators**

I am interested in how the distinctive bodies of harvestmen relate to their behavior and interactions. This collaboration explores questions at the intersection of comparative morphology, behavioral observation, and functional interpretation. It is part of my broader interest in using the diversity of arachnid body plans to investigate how form and behavior evolve together.

## Collaborative research

### Sex-role evolution in *Allocosa* wolf spiders

**With Anita Aisenberg, Leticia, and collaborators**

*Allocosa* wolf spiders offer an opportunity to examine departures from conventional expectations about male and female behavior. Across collaborative projects, I have investigated sexual size dimorphism, mobility, burrow use, and reproductive interactions in South American wolf spiders.

This research asks how ecology and mating systems influence the evolution of sex roles, and how behavioral and morphological variation differs among species and populations. It includes comparative approaches as well as field and laboratory observations.

### Copulatory mechanics and reproductive structures

**With Peter Michalik, Dante Poy, Alfredo Peretti, Camilo Mattoni, and collaborators**

How do reproductive structures actually work during mating? This line of research examines the morphology, movement, and potential functions of structures involved in sperm transfer and copulation. By bringing together anatomical observations, three-dimensional imaging, and behavioral evidence, we aim to understand how structural features interact during reproduction and how they may evolve.

My work on scorpion mating plugs is one example of this approach: it investigates their formation and the contributions of both sexes to a structure whose function depends on the mechanics of mating.

### Evolution of sexual structures in scorpions

**With Alfredo Peretti, Camilo Mattoni, and collaborators**

Scorpions provide a useful system for studying how reproductive traits diversify. I have examined grasping structures, genital traits, mating plugs, and other characters involved in sexual interactions, asking how their variation relates to function, sex differences, and evolutionary history.

This work integrates morphological comparisons with behavioral observations and addresses whether different reproductive structures evolve together or respond differently to selection.

### Sexual selection and reproductive behavior in scorpions

**With Alfredo Peretti, Camilo Mattoni, David Vrech, Paola Olivero, Julieta Giménez Carbonari, and collaborators**

My scorpion research examines mate choice, courtship, sexual communication, reproductive investment, and the functional consequences of morphological variation. One recurring question is how female and male behavior jointly determine the outcome of a mating interaction.

Across projects, I have studied female resistance, male courtship, mating plugs, sexual dimorphism, functional allometry, and changes in reproductive performance. Together, these studies connect fine-scale behavior with broader patterns of sexual selection.

### Physiology and behavior of solifuges

**With David Vrech, Catalina Simian, Eileen Hebets, and collaborators**

Solifuges remain understudied despite their unusual anatomy and reproductive behavior. Our collaborative work examines variation in mating tactics, chemical and mechanical sensing, behavior, and reproductive physiology.

These projects combine behavioral experiments with morphological and physiological approaches to ask how solifuges detect their surroundings, respond to potential mates, and differ between species or sexes.

### Reproductive behavior in pseudoscorpions

**With Ale, Rocío, and collaborators**

Pseudoscorpions add a valuable comparative perspective to my research on arachnid reproduction. This collaboration focuses on reproductive interactions and the diversity of behaviors involved in courtship and mating. Comparing these patterns across arachnid groups can help us identify which features are shared and which may reflect the particular ecology or mating system of a lineage.

### Reproductive interference between scorpion species

**With Luiz Ernesto Costa-Schmidt, Alfredo Peretti, Camilo Mattoni, and collaborators**

What happens when closely related species encounter one another during reproduction? I have studied two scorpion species that occur together and can interfere with each other's sexual communication. This research examines how signals and behavioral responses affect species recognition and mating decisions.

It connects behavioral experiments with questions about coexistence and reproductive isolation: interactions between species may influence both immediate mating outcomes and the longer-term evolution of reproductive traits.

### Geometric morphometrics and scorpion diversification

**With Eduardo Soto, Camilo Mattoni, Alfredo Peretti, and collaborators**

I use geometric morphometrics to examine how the shape of scorpion grasping and reproductive structures varies among species and populations. By comparing multiple traits, we can ask whether morphology changes in a coordinated way or whether different structures follow distinct evolutionary trajectories.

This work has been particularly useful for investigating scorpions that coexist and experience reproductive interactions, linking quantitative shape variation with behavioral and evolutionary questions.
